Learn F1

A beginner's guide to Formula 1: track talk, tyres, the steering wheel and the tech in the 2026 cars.

Everything you hear on a race broadcast, explained with moving pictures. For the full interactive version, with a 3D steering wheel you can spin and click, open Learn F1 on Pit Wall.

What is Formula 1?

Formula 1 is the world's fastest racing championship. Eleven teams build their own cars from scratch, pushing engineering, aerodynamics, materials and software as far as the rules allow, and 22 drivers race them at 23 Grands Prix around the world in 2026. The first World Championship race was held at Silverstone in 1950.

Pushing everything to the limit

Grip like glue

At speed, the air pressing down on the car (downforce) is more than its own weight. In theory, an F1 car could drive upside down on a tunnel roof.

Heavy braking

Drivers brake from over 300 km/h for a hairpin in a few seconds, feeling more than 5 g. That's like having five times your body weight pushing you forwards into the belts.

Brains on board

Hundreds of sensors stream data back to the garage and the factory live, and a driver makes thousands of gear changes in one race.

Red-hot brakes

Carbon brake discs glow at around 1,000°C, and the tyres only work properly once they're hot, which is why drivers weave behind the safety car.

Greener power

From 2026 the engines run on 100% sustainable fuel, and almost half the power comes from electricity recovered while braking.

Built in a wind tunnel

Every surface is shaped by thousands of hours of simulation and tests with 60% scale models, and teams bring new parts to almost every race.

Anatomy of an F1 car

Front wing
The first part to meet the air. It makes downforce for the front tyres and steers the airflow around the rest of the car. In 2026 its flaps move: steep in corners, flatter on the straights.
Nose & crash structure
Holds the front wing and is built to crush in a big frontal impact, absorbing energy before it reaches the driver.
Halo & cockpit
The titanium halo protects the driver's head. The driver sits in a carbon-fibre tub, the survival cell, with their feet higher than their hips.
Sidepods
Hold the radiators that cool the engine, battery and electronics. Their shape is one of the biggest differences between the teams' cars.
Power unit
Behind the driver: a 1.6-litre V6 turbo plus the MGU-K electric motor and the battery, about 1,000 hp together in 2026.
Floor & diffuser
The underside of the car makes a big share of its downforce, sucking it towards the track. The diffuser at the back helps the air escape quickly.
Rear wing
Makes downforce for the rear tyres. In 2026 its flap opens on the straights (straight mode) to cut drag, replacing the old DRS.
Tyres
18-inch wheels with Pirelli tyres: narrower in 2026. The coloured stripe shows the compound: red soft, yellow medium, white hard.

How a race weekend works

Friday: Practice 1 and 2. Saturday: Practice 3, then Qualifying. Sunday: the Grand Prix, about 305 km. Qualifying is a knockout: in Q1 the slowest six of the 22 cars are out, in Q2 another six, and the final ten fight for pole position in Q3. The top ten in the race score 25, 18, 15, 12, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2 and 1 points. On the six sprint weekends of 2026, Practice 2 and 3 are replaced by Sprint Qualifying and a Saturday Sprint of about 100 km, with points for the top eight.

A brief history of F1

  • 1950: The first World Championship race. Silverstone, 13 May 1950. Nino Farina wins it and becomes the first world champion, for Alfa Romeo.
  • 1958: A title for the teams. The Constructors' Championship begins, won by Vanwall.
  • 1959: Engines move behind the driver. Cooper wins the title with a rear-engined car. Within a couple of years, every car follows.
  • 1962: The monocoque. Lotus builds the 25 around a stiff, light one-piece tub, the basic idea every F1 car still uses.
  • 1967: The Cosworth DFV. A Ford-badged V8 wins first time out at Zandvoort and goes on to power much of the grid for more than a decade.
  • 1968: Wings and sponsors. Wings appear to push the cars into the track, and teams swap national colours for sponsor liveries.
  • 1977: Turbo power. Renault brings a turbocharged engine; by the mid-1980s, turbo cars have well over 1,000 hp in qualifying.
  • 1978: Ground effect. Lotus shapes the whole underside of the car to suck it onto the road, and dominates the season.
  • 1981: Carbon fibre. McLaren's MP4/1 has the first carbon-fibre chassis: lighter, stiffer and far safer in a crash.
  • 1989: Paddle shifts. Ferrari introduces the semi-automatic gearbox with paddles behind the steering wheel.
  • 1994: Imola changes everything. Roland Ratzenberger and Ayrton Senna are killed at Imola. The sport's huge safety push that followed has saved many lives since.
  • 2004: Schumacher's fifth in a row. Michael Schumacher and Ferrari win five straight titles from 2000 to 2004; he finishes with a record seven.
  • 2009: KERS. Cars can store braking energy in batteries for the first time: the start of hybrid F1.
  • 2014: The hybrid era. 1.6-litre V6 turbo hybrid power units arrive, among the most efficient engines ever built.
  • 2018: The halo. Titanium head protection becomes compulsory. Two years later it helps Romain Grosjean survive a fiery crash in Bahrain.
  • 2020: Seven titles for Hamilton. Lewis Hamilton equals Michael Schumacher's record of seven world championships.
  • 2021: A budget cap. A cost cap limits how much teams can spend, to close the gap between the biggest and smallest.
  • 2022: Ground effect returns. New cars use shaped floors again so they can follow each other more closely.
  • 2026: A new era. Active aero, a near 50:50 split of engine and electric power, 100% sustainable fuel, smaller and lighter cars. And Kimi Antonelli, aged 20, becomes the youngest champion ever.

Track talk

Racing line & apex

The fastest way through a corner

Drivers use the whole width of the track: they start a corner on the outside, clip the apex (the innermost point) and let the car run back out to the edge on the exit. This outside–inside–outside path makes the corner as gentle as possible, so the car can carry more speed.

Kerbs

The red and white strips at the edge

Kerbs are the striped strips along the inside and outside of corners. Drivers ride over them to straighten the line and gain time, but they're bumpy. Some corners have tall sausage kerbs behind them to stop drivers cutting the corner.

Chicane

A quick left-right (or right-left)

A chicane is a tight flick of corners, usually added to slow cars down before a fast section. Drivers brake hard, hop over the kerbs and fire out the other side. Monza's first chicane comes at the end of a straight where the cars hit well over 300 km/h.

Hairpin

A tight 180° turn

A hairpin turns the cars right around. It's the slowest corner type and a favourite overtaking spot, because drivers brake so hard into it. Monaco's Fairmont hairpin is the slowest corner in F1: Kimi Antonelli's 2026 pole lap went through it at just 44 km/h.

Braking zone

Where the stopping happens

Before a slow corner, drivers brake from over 300 km/h with more than 5 g of force. Boards on the side of the track count down the distance to the corner (often 300, 200 and 100 m) as reference points. Braking even a few metres later than a rival can win a place.

Slipstream (tow)

Hiding in the car ahead's wake

A car punches a hole in the air. Following closely in its slipstream means less drag, so the car behind gains speed on the straight and can pull out to overtake. In qualifying, drivers sometimes use a team-mate's tow on purpose.

Run-off & gravel trap

Space to stop when it goes wrong

Outside many corners there's a run-off area: either paved asphalt or a gravel trap. Gravel slows a car quickly and keeps it out of the barriers, but a driver who goes in there is usually stuck. Asphalt run-off lets them rejoin, which is why track limits are policed so closely.

Track limits

The white lines are the edge

The white lines at the edge define the track. If all four wheels go fully over the line, the driver has left the track. In qualifying that lap time is deleted; in a race, repeated offences bring a warning and then a penalty.

Pit lane

Tyres changed in about two seconds

The pit lane runs alongside the main straight. Drivers must slow to the pit speed limit (usually 80 km/h) using the limiter button on the steering wheel, stop in their box for a tyre change that takes around two seconds, then rejoin. A stop costs about 20 seconds overall, so its timing is a big part of strategy.

Sectors & timing colours

Purple, green and yellow

Every lap is split into three sectors. On timing screens a purple sector is the fastest anyone has gone, green is the driver's own best, and yellow is slower than their best. Three purples in a row usually means pole position.

Virtual safety car (VSC)

Everyone slows down, at the same time

When marshals need to work beside the track but a full safety car isn't needed, race control calls a virtual safety car. There's no car on track: every driver must slow to a set speed, keeping a delta time on their steering wheel positive, and nobody can overtake. Because the gaps between cars stay roughly the same, it's fairer than a safety car, and a pit stop under VSC costs less time than usual. The VSC was brought in for 2015, after Jules Bianchi's crash at Suzuka in 2014.

Flags

How race control talks to drivers

Marshals and the lights on the steering wheel use flags. Yellow: danger, slow down, no overtaking. Red: session stopped. Blue: a faster car is about to lap you, let it by. Green: track clear. Chequered: the end. A safety car or virtual safety car slows the whole field after an incident.

Tyres

Pirelli brings five dry compounds in 2026, C1 (hardest) to C5 (softest), and picks three for each weekend: the hardest is called the Hard (white), the middle the Medium (yellow) and the softest the Soft (red). Intermediates (green) and full Wets (blue) are for the rain. In a dry race every driver must use at least two different dry compounds. The 2026 tyres stay on 18-inch wheels but are narrower: 25 mm at the front and 30 mm at the rear.

Soft

Red stripe

The fastest tyre over one lap and the quickest to warm up, which makes it the qualifying favourite. It wears out fastest, so race stints on it are short.

Medium

Yellow stripe

The all-rounder. A little slower than the soft but lasts longer, so it's often the race-start tyre or the bridge in a one-stop strategy.

Hard

White stripe

The most durable dry tyre and the slowest to come up to temperature. Teams use it for long stints, and on cold days drivers can struggle to make it work.

Intermediate

Green stripe

For a damp or drying track. Shallow grooves clear standing water, but on a dry track the tread overheats and wears out very quickly.

Full wet

Blue stripe

For heavy rain. Deep grooves channel huge amounts of water out from under the tyre to stop the car aquaplaning. It's only needed when there's a lot of standing water.

The steering wheel

A modern F1 steering wheel has 20 or more buttons, dials and paddles. These are the ones you'll hear about most. Spin and click the 3D wheel.

Display
The screen shows the gear, speed, lap delta, battery charge and warnings. Drivers flick between pages to see tyre temperatures, fuel and settings.
Shift lights
A row of LEDs across the top lights up green, red then blue as the engine reaches the perfect moment to change gear. They also flash for flags and warnings.
PIT (pit limiter)
Caps the car's speed to the pit lane limit, usually 80 km/h. Drivers press it just before the pit entry line; speeding in the pit lane brings a penalty.
RADIO
Push to talk to the race engineer on the pit wall. Everything a driver says can be broadcast on TV.
OT (Overtake)
New in 2026: when the driver is within one second of the car ahead, this unlocks extra electrical energy for an overtake on the next straight.
BOOST
Deploys stored battery energy on demand, to attack or to defend. Use too much and the battery runs flat at the end of the straight.
N (neutral)
Selects neutral. Used when the car stops on track or is being pushed in the garage.
DRINK
Pumps a sip of drink through a tube into the helmet. Drivers can lose a few kilos in sweat during a hot race.
OK
Acknowledges messages and confirms changes made in the menus.
MARK
Drops a marker in the data so engineers can find the exact moment the driver felt something odd, like a vibration or a snap of oversteer.
BBAL (brake balance)
Moves braking effort between the front and rear wheels. Drivers adjust it corner by corner, and as fuel burns off and tyres wear.
DIFF
Changes how much the rear differential locks, separately for corner entry, mid-corner and exit. More open helps the car turn; more locked gives stability and traction.
STRAT (power unit mode)
Switches the power unit's mode: maximum power for qualifying, race modes, saving fuel or recharging the battery. Engineers often call out which mode to use.
Upshift paddle
The right paddle behind the wheel changes up a gear. Shifts take a few milliseconds and never cut the power.
Downshift paddle
The left paddle changes down a gear. Under braking drivers click down several gears in a fraction of a second.
Clutch paddles
Two lower paddles work the clutch, used mostly at the start. Drivers hold one at the perfect bite point while the lights come on, then release it when they go out.

Car tech

Power unit

New for 2026

Behind the driver sits a 1.6-litre V6 turbo engine paired with an electric motor, the MGU-K. From 2026 the electric side is almost as strong as the engine: about 400 kW from the engine and up to 350 kW from the MGU-K, close to a 50:50 split. The old MGU-H (which recovered energy from the turbo) is gone, and the cars run on 100% sustainable fuel.

ERS: harvest and deploy

Energy Recovery System

Under braking, the MGU-K works as a generator: it slows the rear wheels and turns that energy into electricity stored in the battery. On the straights it runs the other way, giving the car up to 350 kW of extra push. In 2026 the cars recover around 8.5 MJ per lap, nearly double before, so managing the battery is a huge part of a lap. Electric power fades away at very high speed (from 290 km/h, to zero by 355 km/h).

Active aerodynamics

Replaces DRS

The 2026 cars have moving wings, front and rear. In corner mode the wing flaps are steep for maximum downforce and grip. On designated straights drivers switch to straight mode: the flaps flatten to cut drag and raise top speed, then snap back as soon as the driver brakes. It replaces DRS, the rear-wing flap used from 2011 to 2025. Overall, 2026 cars have about 30% less downforce and much less drag than before.

Overtake mode

New for 2026

If a driver is within one second of the car in front at the detection point, they get Overtake mode: extra electrical energy to attack on the next straight. The chasing car keeps the full 350 kW up to 337 km/h, where electric power would normally start fading from 290 km/h. Drivers can also use their stored energy as a boost to attack or defend.

Differential

Inside vs outside wheel

In a corner the outside rear wheel travels further than the inside one, so it has to spin faster. The differential lets the two rear wheels turn at different speeds. An open diff lets the car turn in easily; a locked diff forces the wheels to turn together, giving stability and traction on exit. Drivers change diff settings for corner entry, mid-corner and exit with rotary dials on the steering wheel.

Brakes

Carbon discs at 1,000°C

F1 brakes use carbon discs and pads that work best when they're extremely hot, often around 1,000°C. At the rear, braking is shared with the MGU-K harvesting energy, so a computer (brake-by-wire) blends the two. Drivers adjust the brake balance (how much braking goes to the front versus the rear) several times a lap with a dial on the wheel.

Gearbox

8 gears, seamless shifts

F1 cars have eight forward gears (and one reverse). Shifts are seamless: the next gear is engaged before the last one lets go, so there's no break in power. Drivers change gear with paddles behind the steering wheel, and a row of shift lights tells them exactly when to change up. A typical lap has between 30 and 60 gear changes.

Halo

Head protection since 2018

The halo is the titanium hoop over the cockpit, introduced in 2018 to protect drivers' heads from flying debris and impacts. It's strong enough to hold the weight of about 12 tonnes. It's credited with saving lives, including Romain Grosjean's when his car went through a barrier and caught fire in Bahrain in 2020.

Size & weight

Smaller and lighter in 2026

The 2026 cars are smaller and lighter: the minimum weight is 768 kg (about 30 kg less than before), the wheelbase is about 3,400 mm (200 mm shorter) and the car is about 1,900 mm wide (100 mm narrower). Tyres are narrower too, so the cars are nimbler in tight corners.

The people who make F1

A Grand Prix takes thousands of people: around a thousand or more at each big team, plus the FIA officials, the tyre makers, the medical crews and the marshals.

Driver

22 on the 2026 grid

The star of the show, but also a top athlete. Drivers handle more than 5 g under braking, race for up to two hours in a cockpit that can get hotter than 50°C, and lose a few kilos in sweat. They train their necks to cope with the forces and spend hours in the simulator learning every track.

Team principal

The boss

The team principal runs the whole operation: hundreds or even more than a thousand staff, the budget (capped by the rules), sponsors and the big decisions. On race day you'll see them on the pit wall with a headset, listening to every call, and in front of the TV cameras when things go well or badly.

Race engineer

The voice in the driver's ear

Each driver has a race engineer: their main contact on the radio. They set up the car with the driver over the weekend and, in the race, pass on lap times, gaps, strategy calls, settings changes and warnings. Many driver–engineer pairs work together for years and become famous for their radio exchanges.

Race strategist

Plays chess at 300 km/h

Strategists decide when to pit and which tyres to fit. Before the race they simulate thousands of possible races; during it they watch gaps, tyre wear, weather radar and rivals, ready to react in seconds to a safety car or a shower. A good call can win a race; a bad one can throw it away.

Pit crew

Four new tyres in about two seconds

Around 20 people service the car in a pit stop: three per wheel (gun, tyre off, tyre on), front and rear jack operators, people steadying the car and someone checking it's safe to release. They practise hundreds of stops a season, and the best stops take barely two seconds.

Mechanics

Build it, fix it, rebuild it

Mechanics assemble the cars at every race, change set-ups between sessions, and rebuild them after crashes, sometimes late into the night. Each car has its own crew led by a chief mechanic, and many of them double up as the pit crew on Sunday.

Designers & aerodynamicists

Back at the factory

Led by a technical director, hundreds of designers and aerodynamicists create the car on computers, then test parts in the wind tunnel and with CFD simulations. The rules limit how much wind-tunnel time each team gets, and the teams lower down the championship are allowed more of it.

Data & performance engineers

Hundreds of sensors, live

The car carries hundreds of sensors measuring everything from tyre temperatures to suspension movement. Data and performance engineers watch it live in the garage, while more engineers back at the factory follow along in a mission-control room and feed advice to the track.

Reserve & simulator drivers

Ready if called

Each team has a reserve driver who can step in if a race driver is ill or injured, sometimes at a few hours' notice. Reserve and development drivers spend long days in the team's simulator, testing set-ups and new parts before they ever reach the real car.

Marshals

The volunteers in orange

Hundreds of marshals line every circuit, most of them unpaid volunteers. They wave the flags, clear crashed cars, fight fires and help injured drivers. They're the first people on the scene of every incident, and races can't happen without them.

Race director & race control

Runs the race for the FIA

The FIA's race director runs every session from race control, surrounded by screens showing every camera and timing feed. Race control starts the race, shows the flags and lights, sends out the safety car, stops the session with a red flag, and reports incidents to the stewards.

Stewards

The referees

A panel of stewards decides penalties: time penalties, grid drops, warnings or worse. They look at video, data and team radio, and can call drivers and team managers in to explain themselves. The panel always includes a former racing driver to bring a driver's view.

Safety car & medical car

Slow the field, save lives

When there's danger on track, race control sends out the safety car: a fast road car driven by a professional racing driver. The field must line up behind it at a reduced speed while marshals clear the track. The medical car, with a doctor on board, follows the field on the first lap and races to any big accident.

Tyre engineers

Pressure and temperature

Engineers from the tyre supplier, Pirelli, look after every set of tyres at a race. They fit them to the rims, check pressures and temperatures, and set the minimum pressures every team must run. Each team also has its own tyre engineer to get the rubber into its best working window.
